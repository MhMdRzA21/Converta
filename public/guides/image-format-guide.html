JPG vs. PNG vs. WebP: Which Should You Use?

The short version: it depends on what's in the image

Most people pick an image format out of habit rather than by thinking about what the image actually contains. That works fine most of the time, but knowing the real differences saves you file size, quality, or both — especially if you're uploading to a site with size limits, or trying to keep a website fast.

JPG (or JPEG)

JPG is built for photographs and other images with smooth gradients and lots of color variation. It achieves small file sizes through "lossy" compression — it throws away some image data that the human eye is less likely to notice, especially in areas of subtle color transition like skies or skin tones. The trade-off is that JPG doesn't support transparency, and compressing it too aggressively (or re-saving a JPG repeatedly) introduces visible blocky artifacts, particularly around sharp edges and text.

Best for: photos, camera images, anything with continuous color gradients where small, unnoticeable quality loss is an acceptable trade for a much smaller file.

PNG

PNG uses "lossless" compression — nothing is thrown away, so the image looks pixel-for-pixel identical to the original every time you save it. It also supports transparency (an alpha channel), which is why logos, icons, and screenshots with transparent backgrounds are almost always PNGs. The cost is file size: a PNG of a detailed photograph is typically much larger than the equivalent JPG, because there's no aggressive compression smoothing things over.

Best for: screenshots, logos, graphics with flat colors or sharp text, and any image that needs a transparent background.

WebP

WebP is a newer format built specifically to beat both of the above at their own game — it offers lossy compression that's typically 25–35% smaller than an equivalent-quality JPG, and it also supports lossless compression and transparency like PNG, usually at a smaller file size too. Browser support is now near-universal, which is why most modern websites default to serving WebP images for faster load times. The main downside is compatibility with older software: some older image editors, printers, and legacy systems don't open WebP files directly.

Best for: websites and anywhere file size matters and you control (or can check) what will be opening the image.

A quick decision guide

  • Uploading a photo somewhere with a size limit? JPG.
  • Need a transparent background, or it's a screenshot/logo with sharp edges and text? PNG.
  • Building or optimizing a website, and want the smallest file at good quality? WebP.
  • Sending to someone using older or unfamiliar software, and not sure they can open WebP? JPG or PNG to be safe.
